# Chapter 1: The Weight of Unfallen Snow

The cold in Pogranichny did not merely bite; it occupied the air like a garrison. 

It was twenty-two degrees below zero on the morning of February 14, 2118. A dry, needle-sharp Siberian wind howled down through the granite passes of the southern Sikhote-Alin range, whipping plumes of powder across the wide avenues of the border town. Overhead, the thick, concrete-insulated mains of the municipal *Teploset* hissed steadily, venting white plumes of superheated steam into the pale grey dawn. Three months after the Time Ripple had shattered the planet’s high-frequency electronics, the physical arteries of the socialist state were humming with stubborn, mechanical defiance. Heavy diesel-electric freight trains still rumbled along the Grodekovo junction toward China, their deep horns reverberating off the snow-blanketed hills.

Luka Huo pulled the heavy wool collar of his coat up to his cheekbones, his gloved fingers clutching a small amber plastic bottle deep inside his right pocket.

Inside the bottle were sixty white neuro-stabilizer tablets, prescribed three days prior by a weary physician at the district polyclinic who had listened to Luka’s symptoms, stamped a slip with an analog rubber seal, and diagnosed him with "Post-Displacement Vestibular Hallucinosis."

*Take two before sleep,* the doctor had muttered, not even looking up from his paper ledger. *Your synapses are just lagging behind your inner ear. Half the district feels like they’re walking on an incline. It passes.*

It had not passed.

For ninety days, ever since the Ripple’s invisible wavefront had passed through his dormitory at the Pogranichny Institute of Relativistic Mechanics, Luka had been living in two worlds. Most of the time, he was simply an associate researcher—a twenty-four-year-old theorist who had grown up eating dumplings in Harbin and had returned to his mother’s Far Eastern border to solve four-dimensional tensor equations on green chalkboards. But when he stared too hard at a teacup, he saw the ceramic fragments lying on the floor an hour before his hand ever slipped. When he watched a maglev engine on the test track, his skull throbbed with a sickening, hot pressure behind his optic nerves, and he saw the charred carbon brushes three weeks before they burned out.

And then there was Aleksey.

Every time Luka sat across from his best friend in the faculty cafeteria, watching Alex laugh, watching Alex casually pour condensed milk into strong black tea with those broad, grease-stained engineer’s hands... Luka’s vision flickered. In a blinding, agonizing flash, the warm cafeteria melted away. In its place, Luka saw a dark, snow-choked industrial clearing. He saw Alex standing in the blizzard, weeping, his face pale and ruined by an unbearable horror, his fingers dripping with fresh, dark crimson blood as he stared down at a motionless body in the snow.

Luka had tried to rationalize it. He had taken the pills. He had stayed awake for thirty-six hours checking his own brainwave equations. But this morning, when Alex had cheerfully knocked on his dorm door to offer him a hot meat pie before the morning seminar, Luka had looked at his best friend’s smiling face and felt a terror so suffocating that he had nearly vomited into the sink.

He could not live with the phantom blood any longer.

***

The Pogranichny District Department of Internal Affairs—the ROVD—was housed in a sturdy, four-story neoclassical stone building on October Square, constructed during the mid-twentieth century and modernized with thick, aerogel-insulated storm windows. Above the heavy oak double doors, a carved bronze emblem of the Soviet Union caught the pale morning light, framed by two frosted sodium lamps.

Luka pushed the door open, grateful for the sudden wall of dry, radiator-driven heat that enveloped him. 

The foyer smelled of wet wool, floor wax, tobacco, and the faint, unmistakable aroma of black currant herbal tea. It was eerily quiet. In the center of the reception hall sat a massive wooden intake counter protected by reinforced glass, behind which stood rows of green metal filing cabinets, an analog wall clock ticking rhythmically, and a darkened *LOGOS-3* ternary data terminal displaying a green phosphor cursor blinking in idle standby.

Behind the desk sat a lone militia officer.

She was young—perhaps twenty-three or twenty-four, with sharp, expressive hazel eyes, dark chestnut hair cropped neatly above her uniform collar, and a face that was currently twisted into an expression of profound, soul-crushing boredom. She wore the dark blue wool tunic of the MVD with red collar piping, and on her shoulder boards sat a single red vertical stripe bearing one small golden star: a Junior Lieutenant. A red enamel badge pinned to her breast pocket read: **ДЕЖУРНЫЙ** (*Duty Officer*).

She was balancing a mechanical brass pencil between her upper lip and her nose, staring intently at the ceiling.

As the heavy entrance door slammed shut, the pencil dropped onto the desk with a sharp *clatter*. She sat up straight in an instant, smoothing her lapels and adopting an expression of severe, textbook procedural authority.

"Pogranichny District Department," she announced, her voice crisp and clear. "Junior Lieutenant Morozova. State your business, citizen."

Luka hesitated, standing in the center of the linoleum floor, melting snow dripping from his boots. "I... good morning, Comrade Junior Lieutenant. I need to file a report."

Nadya pulled a thick, string-bound ledger toward herself, picking up an ink pen. "Lost property, municipal heating dispute, or transit clearance?"

"None of those." Luka swallowed, his throat dry. "I need to report a crime."

Nadya raised an eyebrow, the pen hovering over the fresh page. "A crime? What kind? A warehouse inventory discrepancy?"

"A homicide."

The word hung in the warm air of the foyer like a block of ice.

Nadya froze. She set the pen down slowly, her hazel eyes narrowing as she scrutinized Luka from behind the glass. In a society where basic needs had been universal for nearly seventy years, the word *homicide* carried the weight of an alien invasion.

"Citizen," she said, her tone shifting from routine bureaucracy to cautious evaluation. "Who was killed? Where is the body?"

"No one," Luka said softly. "Not yet."

Nadya blinked. "Excuse me?"

"The crime hasn't happened yet," Luka said, taking a step closer to the counter, his voice trembling with a mixture of exhaustion and desperate earnestness. "I know how this sounds. I know you think I belong in the neurological ward at the polyclinic. But I have... since the Ripple, I have developed an anomalous cognitive condition. When I focus on someone, I can perceive their future worldline. And for the past three months, I have been seeing a murder."

Nadya stared at him in complete silence for five seconds. The wall clock ticked loudly: *tock, tock, tock.*

Then, she let out a long, slow breath, leaning back in her swivel chair and resting her chin in her hand. "Citizen... Huo, is that what your badge says? Associate Researcher Huo from PIRMGD?"

Luka looked down at his coat, realizing his plastic institute security pass was still pinned to his lapel. "Yes."

"Comrade Huo," Nadya said gently, her voice tinged with the weary patience of someone who had dealt with too many post-Ripple dizzy spells this winter. "You are an educated man. You work with tensors and quantum mechanics. Do you know what Article 14 of the Criminal Procedural Code says about evidence?"

"It requires tangible physical or documented proof," Luka muttered.

"Precisely. It does not contain a clause for *'bad dreams had by physicists.'* If I log a report stating that Citizen A is going to commit a hypothetical crime at an unspecified time in an unspecified location based on an unverified temporal vision, do you know what my Senior Investigator will do to this ledger? He will throw it at my head."

"It's not a dream!" Luka’s voice cracked, and he slammed his hand against the wooden ledge of the counter. "I saw the blood, Comrade Lieutenant! I saw him holding the tool! I am not trying to get him arrested—I am trying to stop him from destroying his life! If you could just take down his name, if the department could just monitor his transit passes or—"

"Alright, alright, calm down," Nadya said, waving her hands in a soothing gesture. She studied the dark circles under Luka’s eyes, the pale tremor in his fingers, and the genuine, unfeigned agony in his face. She sighed, her sharp expression softening slightly. 

"Look at me, Comrade Huo," she said suddenly.

Luka blinked. "What?"

"You say you see the future when you focus on a person," Nadya said, crossing her arms and resting them on the desk, a faint, challenging smirk playing at the corner of her lips. "Fine. Look at me. Right now. Focus as hard as that theoretical brain of yours can manage. Tell me what my future looks like. If you get it right, I will personally write down your report, stamp it with the official seal, and file it in the active registry. If you get it wrong, you go home, drink some hot tea, and take the pills in your pocket."

Luka stared at her. "You won't believe me anyway."

"Try me."

Luka took a deep breath. He closed his eyes for a second, feeling the familiar, nauseating pressure building behind his temples, then opened them and locked his gaze onto Junior Lieutenant Nadezhda Morozova.

He stopped looking at the brass buttons of her uniform. He stopped looking at the smooth curve of her jaw. His mind reached forward along the 4D ribbon of her timeline, stretching his conscious perception into the unspooled paper of her future.

A sharp, electric stab of pain shot through his frontal lobe. His vision flared white.

*The sound of applause. A brightly lit auditorium with parquet floors. A large red satin banner hung across the stage, embroidered with gold thread: **«ЧЕСТВОВАНИЕ ПЕРЕДОВИКОВ ОВД ПРИМОРСКОГО КРАЯ — 2118»** (Honoring the Foremost Officers of the Primorsky Krai OVD — 2118). On the stage stood Nadya, her hair slightly longer, wearing a crisp new dress uniform with three stars on each shoulder board—a Senior Lieutenant—smiling with radiant, unabashed pride as a medal was pinned to her lapel.*

Luka blinked hard, gasping for air as the warm foyer of the precinct snapped back into focus. He swayed slightly, catching the edge of the wooden counter to steady his footing.

"Well?" Nadya asked, an amused twinkle in her hazel eyes. "Did I become the Minister of Internal Affairs?"

Luka rubbed his throbbing forehead with the palm of his glove. "You get promoted this year."

Nadya let out a sudden, startled laugh that echoed off the high ceiling. "A promotion? This year? Comrade Huo, that alone proves you are making this up."

"I saw the banner," Luka insisted quietly. "The annual commendation ceremony for Primorsky Krai. You had three stars on your boards. Senior Lieutenant."

"I have been a Junior Lieutenant for exactly eleven months," Nadya said, shaking her head with a grin. "Standard time-in-grade is two years minimum. Unless I personally apprehend an imperial spy slipping across the border on a motorized sled, nobody in Vladivostok is skipping me straight to Senior Lieutenant before December. But..." 

She stopped, looking at Luka’s exhausted, dead-serious face. She picked up her pen again.

"You really believe it, don't you?" she murmured.

"With all my soul," Luka whispered.

Nadya sighed softly and opened the ledger to a fresh blank page. "Sit down on that bench, Comrade Huo. I’ll note the details of your statement. What is the name of your friend, and what exactly did you see?"

***

Before Luka could answer, the heavy front doors swung open with a blast of freezing mountain air.

A stocky, broad-shouldered militia officer in his late fifties stomped into the foyer, his heavy sheepskin *tulup* dusted with snow. He pulled off his thick mittens and slapped them against his thigh, his breath steaming.

"Brrr! Devil take that northern pass," the older officer grumbled in a hearty, gravelly voice. "The asphalt is like a mirror, and the wind could strip the paint off a tram."

"Uncle Senya!" Nadya called out, immediately perking up. "You're back from the mountain road already?"

Senior Sergeant Semyon Korobov pushed back his fur ushanka, revealing a round, ruddy face and a thick grey mustache. "Just came down with the second tow-van. The road is clear up to the 12th kilometer, but Major Tarasov is still up there pacing the snowbanks like a wolf."

Nadya leaned over the counter, her eyes wide with undisguised excitement. "Is it really his car? Igor Romashin’s?"

"The very same," Uncle Senya said, walking behind the desk and heading straight for the electric kettle. "A custom *Russo-Balt Vector*. Burnt right down to the frame. The engine block is melted into the chassis, and the fellow inside... well, looks like a piece of charcoal from a steam boiler. Not a pretty sight."

Nadya practically bounced on her heels. "Uncle Senya... dear, wonderful Semyon Ivanovich... please tell me you'll cover the desk for two hours."

Korobov paused, a glass mug of hot water in his hand, squinting at her suspiciously. "Morozova, you have four hours left on your shift."

"I know, but everyone is up there!" Nadya pleaded, clasping her hands together. "Major Tarasov, Captain Borya, the forensic techs from the district clinic... A high-profile provincial fugitive found burnt to a crisp in our district? This is the biggest case in Primorsky Krai in thirty years, and I’m stuck here stamping lost-cow permits!"

Korobov chuckled, shaking his head. "Tarasov will skin you alive if you show up without an order."

"I’ll tell him I brought extra evidence envelopes!" Nadya turned her most dazzling, desperate smile on him. "Just ninety minutes, Uncle Senya. I’ll bring you a jar of my grandmother’s pickled wild garlic."

Korobov sighed, defeated by the garlic. "Fine, fine. Ninety minutes. But if Colonel Zaytsev steps out of his office, I'm telling him you had a sudden nosebleed." He glanced over the partition at Luka, who was still standing quietly by the bench. Korobov smirked, nudging Nadya with his elbow. "And who’s this? Is that your new boyfriend you’ve been keeping quiet about?"

Nadya rolled her eyes so hard it looked painful. "Ugh, Uncle Senya, for the hundredth time, didn't I tell you that I like women?"

Korobov let out a booming laugh, waving a dismissive hand as he sat down in her swivel chair. "I don't know, Nadya, just in case you realized you were bisexual!"

"Very funny," Nadya muttered, snatching her warm navy-blue winter coat and fur hat from the coat rack. She strapped her leather duty belt around her waist, checked her holster, and turned toward the exit. 

Then, she stopped. She looked at Luka, who was still standing by the door, looking lost, tired, and clutching his pocket.

Nadya tilted her head, a sudden spark of reckless curiosity in her eyes.

"Hey, Comrade Huo," she called out.

Luka looked up. "Yes?"

"You're a spacetime theorist, right? And you claim you can see the history of objects and people?"

"Their future," Luka corrected quietly.

"Whatever," Nadya said, pulling on her thick leather gloves with a decisive snap. "There’s a burnt-out luxury sports car and an unidentified corpse sitting in a snowbank three kilometers up the northern pass. Since you have nothing better to do today than wait for your pills to kick in... you want to see a real crime scene?"

Luka stared at her, caught completely off guard. "Me? But... I'm a civilian."

"You're an academic consultant," Nadya said with an infectious, confident grin, pushing open the heavy front door. "Come on, theorist. Let's see if that brain of yours is good for anything real."
